

## General features or the Grand Republic ##
* The grand dwarven republics are peaceful, trade focused civilizations, with a strong focus on commerce and cooperation.
* Towns and Hamlets are build the fertile plains of your world, embracing the mountains from their kin originates.
* The Merchants Guild is in charge of the republic, sending their representatives as diplomats around the world. 
* They serve the gods of trade, wealth and order.
* Their peacefull nature of avoiding conflic makes them a good trading partner.
* Republican dwarfs value competition and hardwork, cooperation and commerce over martial prowess and merriment.
* Caravans will arrive in spring, summer and autumn. 
* The options for production of clothing and weapons are expanded and they make use of all type of crops
* Specified naming voor groups, roads, bridges, etc.


## Guide for players ##
* They main feature of this mod is the **extended citizenry system**, called Freeholders. It gives all 7 starting dwarves a special status, granting the rights of extensive demands. The progress from *settler* to *citizen*, *burger* and even *patrician*.
When reaching certain thresholds, they are automatically promoted. When you receive message of such a promotion, check the nobles screen for new demands. 
* Read the description of the positions for more details on the requirements and benefits of each position.  
* Once reaching a certain status, members of the citizenry can no longer be assigned tasks. They are exempt from menial labour. However, any taks that they had been assigned before, are still being done. It can by wise to check this early, and only give certain tasks for them to do. When they have the need to craft something, you can consider assign that labor to them, so they can get busy now and then.
* This intricate promotion system has unfortunately some drawbacks:
  * units get multiple sequential positions, resulting in multiple messages. Their status screen also mentions the history of these promotion paths.
  * in world-gen, this system doesnt work. therefore there are dedicated positions created, to give world-gen sites a representation of the fortress-mode system. 
* Clerks are be able to appoint from certain ranks of the citizenry for tasks like trading (clerk of stores) management (clerk of works), accounting (clerk of accounts). Even more of these positions will be opend up when the settlement grows.
* The lack of military focus limits options for building up an army. But when settlements grows, new options will open up.
* If a diplomat leaves unhappy, that might be one of the two merchant journeymen who arrive with the caravan. You probably didn't had a Clerk of stores assigned. Don't be alarmed, the only thing you missed was an supplemental trade negotiation. 
* There is a functional noble position, called Promotion Trigger. This position has no special features, but can be used to trigger in the game that the noblity postions are evaluated, and setting promotion in motion, if aplied. Please ignore. 

## Starting a new fortress ##
* When starting a new fortress, select a civilization with the name '... Republic ...' and that had the 'Grand merchant-master' as the leader of the civilization. 
* On embark, before you unpause, check the noble screen for the position of 'select broker' and read its decription. 
* Dont worry about missing vanilla nobles positions. When you let your fortress grow, new positions will open up automatically.

## Lore
It all began with the charter.

A small company of settlers stepped onto the land, their names inked on a document that promised more than property. The soil beneath their boots was no longer empty earth—it was a freehold, held together by law, by promise, and by their hands. Every stone they laid, every timber they raised, was a claim on the future. These settlers were the first freeholders, and in that status lay both their responsibility and their authority.

Commerce stirred almost immediately. Traders arrived with heavy packs and sharper eyes. Deals were struck in the town square, in the shade of half-built halls, over mugs of ale. Promises had weight. Some merchants faltered; others proved trustworthy. Apprentice clerks ran errands and delivered messages, learning the rhythms of accounts and supplies. Clerks of stores oversaw goods, clerks of accounts counted coins, clerks of letters carried instructions from office to office. Every reliable hand built trust.

From that trust, offices emerged. Trade agents and administrators stepped forward, appointed by the Merchants’ Guild to organize production, enforce rules, and issue mandates. A careful negotiator who resolved a quarrel between neighbors earned a place at council meetings. Homes grew taller, workloads shifted, and the settlement’s heartbeat became both commerce and governance.

Only those descended from, or formally welcomed into, the original freeholders could claim citizenship. Everyone else could trade, prosper, and influence in subtle ways, but the charter marked a boundary: the right to speak, to decide, to govern rested on origin. Citizenship was earned through service, but anchored in lineage. From these citizens rose burghers, then patricians, each layer richer, more secure, and more visible in the city’s life.

The city expanded, and the responsibilities of merchant leaders grew. The merchant master councillor dictated trade policy; the magistrate upheld the law; the ambassador received foreign envoys; the steward managed resources and taxes. Grand commissioners oversaw entire trade networks, while apprentices learned to support the machinery of the guild. Influence accumulated slowly—through trust kept, disputes settled, promises honored—until reliance hardened into expectation.

Institutions arose to preserve order. Guards patrolled streets; bailiffs maintained law; executioners enforced it. Contracts and wealth were protected. Citizens appointed clerks to manage supply and accounts; burghers ensured safety and prosperity; patricians enjoyed comfort and deference, their estates and expectations echoing decades of continuity. All these layers, from settlers to patricians, were tied to the freehold—the land that bore the city, and the authority of those who had first claimed it.

By the time the republic matured, streets thrummed with trade, halls rang with debate, and homes carried the weight of legacy. What began as a simple charter had grown into a living republic: stratified, disciplined, and confident that the hierarchy reflected both founding right and proven competence. The freeholders—the settlers, citizens, burghers, and patricians—remained the true owners of this land, their authority rooted in toil, trust, and tradition.
