## Government structure ##

The Pact does not govern. It coordinates — and only barely. There is no head of state, no legislature, no common law, no institution with authority over its members. What passes for government is five envoys, each carrying a ceremonially weighted name and a single narrow brief: one to receive foreign dignitaries (with appropriate gravity), one to conclude hostilities, one to codify agreements, one to make introductions, one to sustain the distant commerce that makes the whole arrangement worth maintaining. That is the complete formal architecture of the Slavers' Pact. Internal matters — disputes, succession, order within a domain — remain the private business of each master.

## Society ##

The society within each site is organized around a single, openly stated premise: that some command and others serve, and that this is not a misfortune but a design.

The **master** sits at the center of every Pact settlement — sovereign, unaccountable, recognized by the land they hold. Around them, a staff of appointed functionaries run the domain as a private estate: an **exchequer** guards the accounts, a **factor** manages incoming trade, a **taskmaster** drives production, a **physicker** sustains the population's working condition, and an **arbiter** — with constables — resolves what other instruments cannot. These are not really civic offices but rather household functions scaled to the size of a holding.

Outside the master's direct authority, and prior to it in a civic sense, stand the **freemen**: the citizen class whose foundational privilege is exemption from labor. They mark the visible boundary between those who own their time and those who do not. The slave economy below them is its precondition to their status.

A settlement's growth is measured in the personal prosperity of its master. The **patron** title system advances through ten honorifics of escalating grandeur — from *high patron* to *patron ascendant* — each one a declaration of standing rather than a grant of new powers. Prestige, in the Slavers' Pact society, is treated as a tangible good, displayed as deliberately as any other form of wealth.

The **tribune** holds military command, and **legate captains** carry it beyond the settlement's boundaries. Where persuasion fails, force follows. A neighboring territory that has not yet submitted may yet.

When a settlement grows large enough to warrant it, the population elects a **spiritual leader** — the one democratic act in a world otherwise governed entirely by appointment and inheritance — to speak to the twin obligations the Pact regards as sacred: discipline and wealth.

## Lore ##

The Slavers' Pact did not emerge from a founding ideology or a shared myth of origin. The earliest masters held a practice in common before they held anything else: the systematic use of bound labor as the economic foundation of their settlements. In the arid tropics and beneath desert rock, the question was never whether to exploit available resources, but who would do the exploiting. Those who answered that question in the same way found, eventually, that their interests aligned.

The Pact formed around that alignment. Masters whose trade routes overlapped, whose caravans crossed the same hostile terrain, whose slave populations sometimes drew from the same sources — these were convenient partners. The institutional result was deliberately minimal: five envoys, each with a single mandate, none authorized to speak to anything internal. The Pact extended to exactly the point where shared interest required coordination.

Within each settlement, the social structure settled into a consistent form across the Pact's membership. The master held the domain. Freemen occupied the tier above bound labor, their status defined by exemption from it. Appointed functionaries gave the estate its operational coherence. What is notable to the outside observer is how little this hierarchy appears to have been contested. The patron title system — ten honorifics of escalating grandeur, conferred as a domain grew — suggests a society that redirected competitive ambition into status display rather than into political challenge. Power was accumulated, exhibited, and passed on.

The cultural output of Pact settlements is difficult to dismiss. The craftsmanship is accomplished. The arts are practiced with evident investment. Public standards of eloquence and decorum are maintained with apparent sincerity. The Pact understood, at some collective level, that a civilization resting on force alone is an unstable one; culture, ritual, and social expectation were part of the architecture of order.

The spiritual leader — elected when a settlement grows large enough to warrant one — represents the single concession to collective self-determination in the Pact's entire social structure. They speak to the obligations of discipline and wealth, the twin foundations the Pact regards as sacred, and nothing else.
